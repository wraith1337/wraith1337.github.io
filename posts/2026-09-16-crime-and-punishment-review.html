The Punishment Starts Before the Axe

Crime and Punishment by Fyodor Dostoevsky

Read cover to cover · 2026-09-16
SPOILER NOTE: This review discusses the murders, confession, Siberian epilogue and final turn toward renewal.
9/10

The murder in Crime and Punishment occurs early. The punishment begins before Raskolnikov ever picks up the axe.

Dostoevsky's great decision is to make the reader live inside the gap between Raskolnikov's theory and his actual nature. He imagines that exceptional people may cross moral boundaries for a higher purpose. He tells himself that killing the pawnbroker could free money for useful acts and prove that he is not one of the ordinary, obedient mass. Yet the crime itself is ugly, improvised and immediately exceeds the theory: Lizaveta enters unexpectedly and becomes a second victim. Raskolnikov barely uses what he steals. The supposedly rational act reveals itself as a feverish experiment in identity.

The novel's physical world keeps exposing that failure. Petersburg is hot, cramped, dirty and airless. Rooms are described like cupboards and coffins. Raskolnikov wanders through crowds while trying to seal himself inside a private idea. His illness after the murders is not a convenient attack of conscience. It is the body refusing the clean separation his mind wants between thinker and act.

Porfiry understands this better than a detective interested only in evidence. Their scenes are psychological contests in which Porfiry keeps giving Raskolnikov room to betray himself. He sees that confession produced merely by proof would not be enough. Raskolnikov must reach the point where isolation is harder to endure than punishment. Porfiry's strongest pressure is therefore not "I can convict you" but "you cannot continue living alone with this."

The surrounding characters are not side plots so much as alternate answers to the same question: what do people do with suffering and power? Luzhin turns generosity into ownership. Svidrigailov follows appetite until freedom becomes emptiness; his suicide is the bleak endpoint of living beyond obligation to others. Razumikhin answers deprivation with work, friendship and practical care. Sonia is repeatedly humiliated by the world but does not use humiliation as permission to despise it.

Sonia can feel too saintly, and the novel often asks women to bear the consequences of men's ideas. Dounia, Pulcheria and Sonia all absorb pain that Raskolnikov generates. Yet Sonia's importance is not that she wins a theological debate. She gives Raskolnikov a form of human solidarity his theory cannot explain. She follows without excusing him. Her presence makes confession possible, but not easy or complete.

That distinction matters in the epilogue. Raskolnikov initially accepts prison without true repentance. He still thinks his deepest error was failure, not murder. The law can sentence him, but it cannot produce moral knowledge on command. His eventual collapse at Sonia's feet is therefore only a beginning. Dostoevsky explicitly refuses to pretend that one emotional moment finishes regeneration. The new life will require years of striving, suffering and a story the book does not tell.

The novel is messy in ways its shorter philosophical summary would not be. It repeats arguments, drives characters into feverish speeches and gives major space to Marmeladov's family, Luzhin's schemes and Svidrigailov's menace. Some of that sprawl slows the narrative. More often it creates the sense of a city in which every private idea spills into another person's life.

The most unsettling part of the book is not that Raskolnikov believes himself extraordinary. It is how recognizable his evasions are: converting other people into abstractions, treating compassion as weakness, and calling failure the only proof that an action was wrong. His punishment is the collapse of those abstractions under the weight of actual faces.

Verdict

A feverish, uneven and piercing novel about the fantasy of moral exemption and the long return from theory to life.
